"Sea views" is a phrase estate agents love. But from how much of the country can you actually see the sea? I traced 360 sight lines from every 1 km square of the United Kingdom, over the hills and valleys, to find out. Standing on the bare ground, the answer is about a quarter of the land.

Nowhere in the UK is more than {{farthest_coast.km|f0}} km from the coast (counting tidal estuaries), so you might think the sea is never far out of sight. But the sea is low and flat, the land gets in the way, and the Earth curves. A beach-front café can lose its view behind a dune, and a hill farm 30 km inland can look straight down a valley to the water. So I did it properly: for each of the UK's {{n.squares|int}} squares of 1 km, I stood an imaginary person in the middle, eyes 1.7 m above the ground, and looked out in every direction, one degree at a time, as far as 50 km.

Three things to keep in mind throughout. Every figure here is an upper bound: the terrain is bare, with no buildings or trees. The headline is resolution-sensitive: sharper terrain lowers it by up to {{z10.max_abs_diff|f1}} points in my test areas. And "sea" means OpenStreetMap's coastline, which includes tidal estuaries and sea lochs.

A quarter of the land

From {{main.uk_km2|int}} of those squares, {{main.uk_pc|f1}}% of the UK's land, there is sea in view. Usually it is close: where you can see the sea at all, the nearest bit you can see is typically about {{near.median_km|f0}} km away. And usually it is a slice, not a panorama. Among squares with a sea view, the median one sees sea across only {{horizon.median_pc_seaview|f0}}% of the horizon. Only {{horizon.over_half_km2|int}} squares look out on sea across more than half of it: headlands, small islands and the tips of peninsulas.

Scotland looks out, England looks in

The nations are very different. In Scotland, with its long, ragged, mountainous coast and its sea lochs reaching deep inland, {{main.Scotland.pc|f0}}% of the land has a sea view. Wales comes next at {{main.Wales.pc|f0}}%, then Northern Ireland at {{main.Northern Ireland.pc|f0}}%. England, with its great lowland middle, manages only {{main.England.pc|f0}}%.

{{IMG|b_nations.png|Two bar charts: the share of land with a sea view by nation for four eye heights, highest in Scotland and lowest in England, and the share of land and of people in Great Britain with a sea view, rising with eye height|Left: land with a sea view by nation and eye height. Right: land and people in Great Britain.}}

What about people? Most of us live in lowland towns, and many coastal towns look out across flat land, estuaries or sea walls. Splitting each constituency's population between its towns and villages, and checking each settlement's own point, {{pop.1.7.gb_pc|f0}}% of people in Great Britain live somewhere the terrain lets them see the sea. In Northern Ireland it is about {{pop.1.7.ni_pc|f0}}% of the settlement population. That counts the land only: in real streets, houses and trees hide much of it.

How far inland?

Almost every square right on the coast sees the sea ({{by_dist.0.pc|f0}}% within a kilometre), but even there not all: {{no_seaview_by_coast.within_2km_pc|f0}}% of squares within 2 km of the coast have no sea view, tucked behind dunes, cliffs or the lie of the land. Further in, height decides. On low ground, below 50 m, half the squares have lost the sea by about {{reach.0 to 50.km|f0}} km inland. On ground of 150 to 300 m the half-way point is about {{reach.150 to 300.km|f0}} km in. Higher still does no better at that half-way mark ({{reach.300+.km|f0}} km), because most land above 300 m is in mountain country, where the next mountain hides the sea; but the high tops keep the sea in view more often further in: {{seaview_far_inland.n_300_40km|int}} squares above 300 m see it from 40 km or more from the coast.

Climb the stairs

Height buys sea. Raise your eyes to an upstairs window, 5 m up, and the share of UK land with a sea view goes from {{main.uk_pc|f0}}% to {{eyes.5.uk_pc|f0}}%. From a fifth floor, 15 m up, it is {{eyes.15.uk_pc|f0}}%, and from the top of a 50 m tower, {{eyes.50.uk_pc|f0}}%. For people it is {{pop.5.gb_pc|f0}}% from an upstairs window. (These heights are applied everywhere, moorland included, so they show what a tall building could see, not what any actual one does.)

The sea-view desert

Somewhere has to be the farthest from any view of the sea. In this model it is a square near {{farthest_seaview.0.near.name}} in {{farthest_seaview.0.county}}, {{farthest_seaview.0.km|f0}} km from the nearest square where you could see the sea. The square farthest from the coast itself is {{farthest_coast.km|f0}} km from it, near {{farthest_coast.near.name}}. Around them lies a wide stretch of inland England where no square for tens of kilometres has a sea view: {{counties.n_none|int}} counties, from Oxfordshire to West Yorkshire, have none at all.

Checking against the hills

To check the model, I took {{validation.n|int}} hills and viewpoints whose sea view is written down somewhere: Black Combe ("half the view is the glittering sea"), Dunkery Beacon over the Bristol Channel, Leith Hill Tower, where the National Trust says you can see the English Channel on a clear day, Snowdon, Scafell Pike, Slieve Donard and others. The model finds the sea from {{validation.terrain_50|int}} of them at the height of the terrain data and from {{validation.published_50|int}} at their real summit heights. The terrain data rounds summits down, and Pendle Hill is the clearest case: at its real height of 557 m the model sees the Lancashire coast about {{validation.points.2.published_50_near_km|f0}} km away, just as George Fox did in 1652, when he wrote that from the top "I saw the sea bordering upon Lancashire". The one it never finds is Worcestershire Beacon, whose "Bristol Channel" view I could not reproduce even looking 100 km out.

How I did this

The land is the open AWS Terrain Tiles, a mosaic of satellite radar and other height data, at about 175 m resolution. The sea is OpenStreetMap's coastline, which runs up tidal estuaries and sea lochs. From the centre of every Ordnance Survey 1 km square in Great Britain, and every Irish Grid square in Northern Ireland, I traced a sight line every degree, in steps of about 90 m, out to 50 km. Each step asks whether the ground, or the sea surface, rises above everything nearer, allowing for the curve of the Earth and the usual slight bending of light near the ground. A sight line stops at the first sea it can see. I wrote the plan, with every check, before running it.

Most of the checks move the headline by a point or so: looking 100 km out, ignoring refraction, leaving out estuaries and narrow sea lochs, needing the sea on at least three sight lines, twice as many sight lines, four points per square, or random errors in the heights. Looking only 25 km out lowers it to {{sens.0.pc|f0}}%. The check that matters most is sharper terrain. With heights every 88 m instead of 175 m, the share falls in all four test areas, by up to {{z10.max_abs_diff|f1}} points (about {{z10.rel_drop_pc|f0}}% of its value overall), because small ridges and dunes that the coarse data smooths away start to hide the sea from low ground. Across all the checks the share stays between {{sens_range.lo_pc|f0}}% and {{sens_range.hi_pc|f0}}%, so the headline is resolution-sensitive and "about a quarter" is, if anything, generous. The biggest caveat cannot be checked with this data. The terrain has no buildings, trees or hedges, which hide the sea from most streets and many fields. So read every figure as an upper bound: where the land itself allows a sea view, the most you could hope for on a clear day.
